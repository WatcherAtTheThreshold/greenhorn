# Green Horn — Story Fragments

*Working notes. Nothing here is settled. Companion to `green-horn-outline.md`.*

---

## The world

Humans are gone. Robots remain, executing a long-term plan to re-establish the biosphere and bring humanity back.

Before the end, humans built a photosynthesis technology meant to repair the atmosphere. It worked, and then it kept working. Oxygen climbed past 35%.

Arthropods recover fastest after mass extinction events. With no predators, no competition, and an atmosphere that lifted every constraint on their size, they established first and grew enormous.

Shares a world and lore with *Subtle Spirits*.

### Why 35% is the right number

Insects breathe by passive diffusion through tracheal tubes rather than lungs, so body size is capped by how deep oxygen can physically reach into the body. Raise the partial pressure and the ceiling rises with it. 35% is roughly the Carboniferous peak — the period that actually produced 70cm dragonflies and two-meter millipedes.

The premise reverse-engineers the real mechanism rather than hand-waving it.

### Two consequences that fall out for free

**Everything burns.** Above roughly 30% oxygen, even damp vegetation ignites and fire spreads through material that shouldn't carry it. The Carboniferous fossil record is full of charcoal for this reason. The world periodically burns itself back — a hazard, a mechanic, and an explanation for a patchy landscape, all without contrivance.

**Oxygen eats robots.** The protagonist doesn't breathe, so atmosphere should be irrelevant to it — except that oxidation scales with oxygen concentration. A world that lets insects grow enormous corrodes machines faster than they can maintain themselves.

Rust becomes run pressure. Not an arbitrary timer: the environment is actively consuming you, and every minute outside shelter costs hull. The same atmospheric fact produces both the enemies and the clock.

## The closed loop

Humans cannot live at 35% oxygen — sustained exposure causes pulmonary oxygen toxicity. The technology built to save humanity is precisely what prevents its return.

The robots cannot complete their purpose until the oxygen comes down. Oxygen comes down when biomass burns and when the runaway growth is cut back.

**Killing the bugs is the terraforming.** The violence isn't a genre convention bolted onto an ecology story — it is the ecology story.

---

## The protagonist

A unit sent out into the gap. It will rust away long before the job is done, get backed up, and be sent out again.

**Death is diegetic.** Robots have backups. A failed run isn't a game over — it's a unit being reactivated with what the last one learned. Meta-progression stops being a genre concession and becomes the story.

**Augments are physical.** Items bolt onto the chassis — shoulder mount, back rack, forearm swap, head module. Fiction, mechanic, and implementation are the same object.

**You wear what you kill.** Beetle carapace becomes shoulder plating. A robot in a dead world has no supply chain — only what it can pry off a corpse. If augments visibly identify which species they came from, the player's build becomes a record of what they killed. No commentary required; they're looking at it on their own robot.

## The twist

**Not a lever.** Not a final choice screen, not two endings, not "decide whether to bring humans back." That version puts all the weight on one moment and lets everything before it go unexamined.

**The stronger version:** the player works it out from mechanics they've been operating the whole time.

Killing bugs lowers oxygen. Lowering oxygen is terraforming. The framing tells the player this is progress. The same numbers also describe a deliberate extinction — one ecosystem cleared so another can be installed. A biosphere already exists. It just isn't the one the specification calls for.

The moment to aim for: a player notices that the healthy-planet meter and the dead-arthropods counter are the same number.

**The robot is not malevolent.** It's executing a completed specification with no capacity to ask whether the specification is still correct. That's a far more familiar kind of harm than villainy.

---

## Miscalculation

The thread that makes the robots tragic rather than sinister. The plan rests on numbers a machine cannot check — a target atmospheric composition drawn from records written by people who are gone, with no way to verify the target is still right, no way to notice the world has moved past the spec, and no one to ask.

Three forms this could take:

**The specification is stale.** The oxygen target was set before the runaway. The robots have been correcting toward a figure that no longer describes anything real. Every step executed perfectly against a wrong constant.

**The math has drift.** Each reactivation copies from a copy. Something small degrades. Your unit may be running a slightly corrupted version of the plan and cannot tell — that's what corruption means. The death system doing thematic work for free.

**They aren't sure humans were the point.** Somewhere in the archive is the possibility that the mission was a directive rather than a conclusion. The robots have no framework for evaluating a directive. They can only follow it or not.

### The juxtaposition

Humans were fragile and knew it — which is why they built successors.

Robots are durable and don't know their own fragility, because their vulnerability isn't physical. It's that they can't revise.

Rust is the visible weakness. Certainty is the real one.

## The pacifist bugs

Some species never attack. Ever, under any circumstance. They flee, or they simply carry on.

They're worth the same oxygen contribution as anything else. The game never comments on killing them or sparing them — no dialogue, no meter, no achievement, no penalty.

**Three rules that make it work:**

1. **Silence is essential.** The moment the game acknowledges the choice, it becomes a mechanic and the player optimizes it. Unacknowledged, it stays a decision.
2. **They have to be worth killing.** If sparing them is free, it isn't a choice. The oxygen math must be genuinely indifferent — a docile beetle counts exactly as much as one trying to kill you.
3. **Make them legible.** Different silhouette, different movement, no attack telegraph. The player should be able to tell within a second, so that killing one is never an accident after the first time.

**The detail worth building:** their shells make the best armor in the game. Something that never evolved a weapon put everything into defense. So the strongest protective augments come off the creatures that weren't going to hurt you.

The player works that out alone, and then keeps doing it.

That's the whole theme in a loot table. No writing required.

### Rule 2 answered itself — 2026-09-03

The worry was that sparing a docile beetle would be *free*, and a choice with
no cost is not a choice. The build answered it before any economy existed,
and better than an economy would have.

**They are in the way.** A harmless beetle is a solid body of exactly the same
size as a dangerous one. In a crowd they can be used as cover — putting one
between you and a biter genuinely works — and they can equally trap you,
because closing on a biter through a press of docile ones means eating a bite
you could otherwise have walked around.

So the reason to kill one is not points. It is that **it was standing where
you needed to be.** That is a far more uncomfortable answer than a number,
and it arrives at no cost: it is emergent from collision, and the game never
says a word about it.

Which sharpens rule 2 rather than satisfying it. Whatever currency eventually
exists, the docile ones do not need to pay *more* to be worth killing. They
already are, for the worst possible reason.

## Lab stations

Gives runs a destination other than a boss door, and a reason to come back.

**The risk:** if the station converts kills into progress, the killing becomes administratively necessary and the discomfort drains out. "The system required it" is the excuse the player was already reaching for — don't hand it to them.

**The version that keeps the friction:** the station doesn't tell you what it's doing. You bring back samples, you insert them, a number moves. Nobody explains which direction is good. The robot doesn't know either — it's a subroutine, not a scientist.

Late in the game you learn what the number was measuring.

Structurally this is Threshold Deep's meta-progression with a fictional skin that actually fits. M4 territory — it only means anything once a run has an end to return from.

---

## Governing principles

**Don't resolve it.** A game that concludes killing is wrong stops being interesting the moment you understand it. A game that makes the killing work well, feel good, and stay quietly unresolved keeps its grip.

**Discomfort has to survive the fun, not replace it.** If the combat isn't satisfying, nobody plays long enough to feel bad about it. Threshold Deep's lesson holds: following one's own ideology in all things doesn't produce the best experience.

**Humans don't have to be the goal.** Room for non-human-centric ideas. Humans as a step rather than a destination is a legitimate outcome, and the story can hold that without arguing for it.

**Nearly all of this is reinterpretation.** A counter, a loot table, an enemy AI flag, a return trip. The thematic layer costs almost nothing to add later because it's mostly a reading of numbers already being tracked. It needs a game underneath it first.

---

## Title

**Green Horn** does three jobs at once:

- *greenhorn* — the novice, the newly activated unit
- *green* — returning plant life, the runaway photosynthesis
- *horn* — mandible, carapace, the things being fought

## Open threads

- What does the robot know about the plan? Full archive access, fragments, or only its immediate directive?
- Is there any other robot to talk to, or is the unit alone? Does the backup system have a voice?
- Does the player ever see a human record directly, or only the robots' interpretation of one?
- What's on the other side of the oxygen number — is there a state where the plan completes, and what does that look like?
- Do the pacifist species appear early enough to establish the pattern before the player has a reason to reconsider it?
- How much does the mission briefing drift between reactivations, and does the player notice it or only feel it?

- **Does rust hand the player the excuse the lab station is supposed to withhold?**
  The lab-station note is right that *"the system required it"* is what the
  player is already reaching for. But rust supplies exactly that: if every
  minute costs hull and a docile beetle is worth real oxygen, killing it is
  efficient, and the player gets to think *I was rusting, I needed it.*
  The alibi is built into the run structure.

  Probable fix, and it is small: make sparing cheap in **time** but real in
  **progress**. Never put a pacifist species in a corridor or a chokepoint. If
  walking past costs nothing but the oxygen you did not take, the decision is
  purely *do I want this*, which is the version with no excuse in it.

- **What makes the world feel alive enough for the twist to land?**
  The theme rests on *a biosphere already exists, it just isn't the one the
  spec calls for.* Mechanically there is nothing that says so — there are
  things that walk at you. For that line to have any weight the player has to
  see an ecosystem being one: creatures eating, nesting, ignoring you,
  reacting to each other rather than to the player.

  The cheap version is not a system, it is an AI state: **bugs that are
  already doing something when you arrive, and stop doing it when you
  engage.** That single behaviour does more for *I am intruding on something*
  than any amount of writing.

  Noticed by accident, not by planning: during M2 there was a build where the
  bug could walk at you but neither of you could attack, and it read
  immediately as a **follower pet** rather than an enemy. Same observation as
  [m2-first-fight.md](m2-first-fight.md) open question 5.
